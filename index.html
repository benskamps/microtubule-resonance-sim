Seven hypotheses, one browser

Start inside the cell: inside every one of your cells are tiny hollow tubes — microtubules — that form part of the cell's scaffolding. A handful of physicists have wondered whether these tubes might “ring” like microscopic antennas at particular frequencies. A few have wondered whether that might have something to do with how the brain works. This simulator tests seven specific versions of that idea with real physics, and reports the ones that failed. One came back statistically insignificant. A plain regular structure outperformed the fancy “fractal” one. Keeping the tubes actively ringing would cost a neuron roughly 16× its entire energy budget. It's a tool for sharpening the question, not a claim that the answer is yes.

Why This Exists

In 2020 and 2022, Anirban Bandyopadhyay and colleagues published findings reporting fractal electromagnetic resonance in single microtubule nanowires. The reported pattern is a self-similar "triplet of triplet" that repeats across four frequency decades (Hz, kHz, MHz, GHz). They also reported that cytoskeletal filaments fire ~250μs before the membrane ionic spike — challenging the classical Hodgkin-Huxley model of neural signaling.

These are extraordinary claims. This simulator models them numerically to see which parts hold up.

This simulator was built to ask: Are these patterns mathematically plausible? Are they internally consistent? Could simpler structures produce the same effects? Not to prove or disprove, but to sharpen the questions that matter before (or alongside) expensive laboratory experiments.

Key Findings

Each hypothesis was tested computationally and assigned a verdict using a revised taxonomy that distinguishes between independent evidence, model self-consistency, and unvalidated claims.

Hypothesis Verdict Summary
H1 Fractal Coherent Amplification Plausible The 36 fractal modes sum coherently to 17.7 vs random ~5.3. A regular lattice reaches 22.9.
H2 Chirality Creates Triplets Inconclusive With every mode counted, the twisted tube does show triplet clusters, but no more than random splittings of the same size produce, and the verdict flips with small pitch and cutoff changes. The test cannot credit the triplets to chirality.
H3 Boundary Dominates Bulk Consistent Sub-linear length scaling confirmed, but this is tautological — the model is confirming its own boundary assumptions.
H4 Filament-First Temporal Ordering Consistent Fast oscillator leads slow oscillator as expected. Also tautological — built into the coupled oscillator structure.
H5 Pitch Angle Optimality Inconclusive Peak at 4°, 8° from the actual 12° pitch, and the position depends on the αcritical parameter. Weakest robustness (55%).

What This Means

The fractal resonance pattern described by Bandyopadhyay is mathematically real but not uniquely special. Simpler ordered structures (like a regular lattice) can produce equal or greater coherent amplification. The specific triplet-of-triplet arrangement is not required: a plain regular lattice scores 22.9 against the fractal's 17.7.

Stochastic resonance (H6), the effect where a specific amount of noise makes a weak signal easier to detect, does not appear in this model: the signal-to-noise ratio only falls as noise rises. The model's drive is faster than its double well can hop at any noise level tested, so there is nothing to resonate with. An earlier version called this plausible; the 2026-10-05 audit traced that to an SNR estimate that counted the well offset as noise.

The energy budget analysis provides a hard constraint: active MHz oscillation in microtubules is energetically implausible (requiring ~40x a neuron's total power budget). Any valid mechanism must be passive. Whether thermal noise could drive it is a rate question (attempt frequency and barrier) the model does not answer.

The Schumann resonance alignment (H7) — perhaps the most provocative claim — does not survive statistical scrutiny. Random fractal patterns match Schumann harmonics almost as well (p=0.183).

The key open question: Are these resonances experimentally measurable at the predicted frequencies? Direct electromagnetic spectroscopy of isolated microtubules at MHz-GHz would settle the core claims in a way no computational model can.

Simplified Hypothesis Guide

Each hypothesis explained without jargon, along with what we tested and what it means.

H1

Fractal Coherent Amplification

Plausible

Imagine 36 tuning forks. If they vibrate in random directions, they partly cancel out (like a crowd all talking at once — you get about 5x louder). If arranged in a specific pattern, they reinforce each other (like a choir singing in harmony — you get 36x louder). Our test found the fractal pattern does amplify the signal, but a simpler regular arrangement actually works slightly better. The nesting structure matters, but the specific fractal pattern is not uniquely optimal.

What we computed: Berry phase accumulation across 36 modes. Fractal = 17.7, regular lattice = 22.9, random average = 5.3, perfect alignment (all phases identical) = 36. Seed 20260906.

H2

Chirality Creates Triplets

Inconclusive

Microtubules are twisted (chiral), like a spiral staircase. The question: does this twist create the triplet resonance pattern? We compared a twisted tube to a straight one, counting every resonance including both directions of rotation. The twist does produce groups of three, but random twists of the same size produce as many, and the answer changes with small parameter changes. So the test cannot say chirality creates the triplets, and it cannot rule it out either.

What we computed: Cylindrical standing wave modes (n from −12 to 12) with and without helical pitch, degenerate pairs counted once, against 100 random-splitting null draws and a 9-point pitch and cutoff grid.

H3

Boundary Dominates Bulk

Consistent

Does the amplification happen at the surface of the tube or throughout its interior? If it is a surface effect, making the tube longer should not help much (the surface-to-volume ratio decreases). Our model shows sub-linear scaling — consistent with a boundary effect. But this is the model confirming its own design, not independent evidence.

What we computed: Amplification factor vs. tube length. Scaling was sub-linear. Caveat: Tautological test — the boundary assumption was built in.

H4

Filament-First Temporal Ordering

Consistent

Classical neuroscience says the membrane fires first, then the cytoskeleton responds. Bandyopadhyay's team measured the opposite: filaments fire 250 microseconds before the membrane. We modeled this as a fast oscillator (filament) coupled to a slow one (membrane). The fast one does lead — but that is what coupled oscillators do when one is faster. The model confirms its own structure, not the biological claim.

What we computed: RK4 integration of coupled ODEs. Fast oscillator phase-leads slow oscillator. Caveat: Expected from the math, not an independent prediction.

H5

Pitch Angle Optimality

Inconclusive

Microtubules have a specific twist angle (~12 degrees). Is this angle special? We swept from 0 to 45 degrees and found a peak at 4 degrees, 8 degrees off the real pitch — and the peak position depends on a free parameter we can tune. With different parameter choices, the peak moves. This is the least robust result (55% stability under perturbation).

What we computed: Berry phase resonance condition and coupling efficiency vs. pitch angle from 0° to 45°. Peak at 4.0° (8° from the 12° pitch), sensitive to αcritical = 15°.

H6

Thermal Noise as Fuel

Falsified

Usually, noise destroys signals. But in certain nonlinear systems, a specific amount of noise makes the signal stronger — stochastic resonance. Its signature is a peak: the signal improves with noise up to a point, then falls. In our model the signal-to-noise ratio only falls as noise rises, so the model does not show stochastic resonance at these settings. The earlier “nine-fold improvement” was an artifact: the old estimate counted the particle sitting in one well as noise, which made quiet runs look noisy.

What we computed: SNR vs. noise amplitude at the three relative drive frequencies (0.7, 1.0, 1.4) in a bistable potential (Euler-Maruyama integration, 500 trials × 16 noise levels), with SNR from a least-squares offset, sine and cosine fit.

H7

Scale Invariance Predicts Schumann

Unvalidated

If the fractal pattern extends to all scales, it should predict Earth's Schumann resonances (electromagnetic standing waves in the atmosphere at 7.83 Hz, 14.3 Hz, etc.). Our extrapolation matched 4 out of 5 harmonics. Sounds impressive — until we ran 10,000 random fractal patterns and found that 18.3% of them match just as well. This is not statistically significant (p=0.183). The apparent alignment may be coincidence.

What we computed: 10,000 Monte Carlo trials with random triplet patterns. Observed 4/5 matches vs. random mean of 2.58±1.02 matches. p=0.183.
